    Reference Architectures

    Why does this matter?

    Individual XRPL primitives are powerful in isolation; their value compounds when composed correctly into a complete system. Reference architectures make the composition explicit — account topologies, data flows, failure modes, and operational runbooks that production systems require. Studying them prevents architects from discovering edge cases in production.

    Mental model

    Every XRPL application can be decomposed into three concerns:

    1. Account topology — which accounts exist, what roles they play, and what signing authority each carries.
    2. Transaction flows — the sequence of on-ledger operations that implement a business function, and how they fail and recover.
    3. Off-ledger integration — how the application connects to traditional systems (databases, fiat rails, compliance tools) while keeping the validated ledger as the source of truth.

    Architecture

    Five reference architectures follow. Each one names its account topology, the primitives it composes (with the xrpld source that defines them), and a runbook for the operations that matter. Paths are relative to the vendored rippled/ tree; the software is xrpld in prose.

    1. Cross-border payment provider

    Move value from a sender in one currency to a beneficiary in another, settling on-ledger via cross-currency payments.

    Account topology. A funded operational account (day-to-day signing via a regular key, master disabled) submits payments; per-customer routing uses DestinationTag rather than one account per customer.

    Primitives. - Cross-currency Payment — src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/payment/Payment.cpp. Pathfinding routes across DEX order books and AMM pools (see ch03 DeFi). - tfPartialPayment (0x00020000, include/xrpl/protocol/TxFlags.h:105) — let the delivered amount fall short rather than fail on a thin path; always read the metadata delivered_amount (include/xrpl/protocol/jss.h:216), never the sent Amount. - LastLedgerSequence on every submission for deterministic expiry; retry logic per ch01 Payments.

    Runbook. Ingest fiat → construct the cross-currency Payment with a bounded SendMax and a LastLedgerSequence a few ledgers out → submit and poll the validated ledger → on success read delivered_amount for the payout leg → on tecPATH_DRY/tecPATH_PARTIAL alert and re-quote. Reconcile fiat in/out against validated deliveries on a schedule.

    2. Stablecoin issuer platform

    Issue a fiat-backed IOU with reserve attestation and regulatory controls.

    Account topology. A cold issuing account (master key, kept offline) sets issuer policy; a hot distribution account (regular key / signer list) does day-to-day sends. This mirrors ch02 Stablecoins.

    Primitives. - Issuer flags via AccountSet — asfRequireAuth, asfDefaultRipple, asfNoFreeze, asfGlobalFreeze, asfAllowTrustLineClawback (include/xrpl/protocol/TxFlags.h; guards in src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/account/AccountSet.cpp). asfRequireAuth and asfAllowTrustLineClawback require zero owner objects first (tecOWNERS, include/xrpl/protocol/TER.h). - TrustSet / RippleState for balances; TransferRate for the transfer fee. - Clawback — src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/token/Clawback.cpp. The sfAmount issuer field is the holder's account, not the issuer — the single most error-prone detail in the whole flow.

    Runbook. Ceremony: on the cold account set asfRequireAuth (before any trust lines) and, if required, asfAllowTrustLineClawback and/or asfNoFreeze; disable the master only after a regular key/signer list exists (see ch04 Security). Steady state: authorize holder trust lines → issue from hot account → reconcile on-ledger supply against reserves → freeze/claw back per policy.

    3. RWA tokenization platform

    Represent a real-world asset as a permissioned token with identity-gated access and a defined lifecycle.

    Account topology. An issuer account owns the MPT issuance; a domain owner account owns a permissioned domain that gates who may hold or trade.

    Primitives. - MPT lifecycle — MPTokenIssuanceCreate (capability flags tfMPTRequireAuth, tfMPTCanTransfer, tfMPTCanLock, tfMPTCanClawback, tfMPTCanEscrow, tfMPTCanTrade), MPTokenAuthorize (auth handshake; tecNO_AUTH / tecNO_PERMISSION), MPTokenIssuanceSet (lock/unlock; DynamicMPT conditional mutability), MPTokenIssuanceDestroy (tecHAS_OBLIGATIONS while sfOutstandingAmount/sfLockedAmount ≠ 0; tecNO_PERMISSION if not issuer) — src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/token/. See ch02 Tokenization. - Identity primitives — ltDID, ltCREDENTIAL, ltPERMISSIONED_DOMAIN (include/xrpl/protocol/detail/ledger_entries.macro); access is gated by binding the issuance's DomainID (which requires tfMPTRequireAuth), not by putting identity inside the token.

    Runbook. Establish issuer identity (DIDSet) → create the permissioned domain listing accepted credentials → issuers/verifiers issue credentials (CredentialCreate → CredentialAccept) → MPTokenIssuanceCreate with DomainID → holders opt in and are authorized → distribute → (secondary trading scoped to the domain) → at maturity redeem to zero, then MPTokenIssuanceDestroy. The ordering is an application pattern; the protocol enforces only the individual gates.

    4. Exchange / custody integration

    Detect deposits, segregate hot/cold funds, and process withdrawals safely.

    Account topology. A deposit account per venue (shared, keyed by DestinationTag), a hot withdrawal account (minimal balance, swept), and a cold reserve account (multi-sig, offline).

    Primitives. - asfDepositAuth (include/xrpl/protocol/TxFlags.h:431) and the asfDisallowIncoming* family to constrain who/what can pay an account. - Hot/cold separation via regular keys and signer lists (src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/account/SignerListSet.cpp) — see ch04 Security. - TicketCreate (src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/system/TicketCreate.cpp) to pre-allocate sequence slots for out-of-order / parallel withdrawal signing. Tickets decouple sequence assignment from signing order, which is essential when the cold signer is offline and multiple withdrawals must be queued.

    Withdrawal signing flow.

    Every withdrawal passes through three stages before broadcast:

    1. Transaction builder (online)
   └─ Construct tx JSON: Amount, Destination, DestinationTag, Fee (from
      server_info), Sequence or Ticket, LastLedgerSequence

2. Codec gate (online, boundary)
   └─ Call: server_definitions { hash: <cached_hash> }
      Match  → forward tx JSON to signer
      Mismatch → HALT: amendment shipped, refresh codec before proceeding
      See [ch04 Custody](../../ch04-hardening/custody/README.md#codec-pinning-workflow)

3. Signer (hot key / cold signer-list quorum, offline)
   └─ Serialize fields in fieldCode order using cached server_definitions
   └─ Sign SHA512Half(0x53545800 || serialized_bytes) with private key
   └─ Return signed tx_blob

4. Submit (online)
   └─ submit RPC with tx_blob
   └─ Poll validated ledger for tx hash; credit only on tesSUCCESS/tec in
      validated ledger

    Below the withdrawal threshold: hot account signs via regular key (online HSM). Above the threshold: cold account signer-list quorum required (M-of-N, each co-signer runs the same codec gate independently before signing).

    Runbook. Credit deposits only after they appear in a validated ledger and match an expected DestinationTag; sweep the hot account to cold on a threshold; sign withdrawals from the hot account (regular key) or, above a limit, require the cold signer-list quorum; reconcile balances against the internal ledger continuously.

    5. DeFi-powered treasury

    Deploy idle treasury assets into on-ledger liquidity with oracle-driven, rules-based rebalancing.

    Account topology. A treasury account (multi-sig) holds positions; automation signs via a scoped regular key with tight monitoring.

    Primitives. - AMM — AMMCreate/AMMDeposit/AMMWithdraw/AMMBid/AMMVote (src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/dex/). Invariant sqrt(asset1 * asset2) >= LPTokensBalance (src/libxrpl/ledger/helpers/AMMHelpers.cpp:47). Trading-fee ceiling kTradingFeeThreshold = 1000 (1%) in units of 1/100000 (kAuctionSlotFeeScaleFactor = 100000), auction slot kTotalTimeSlotSecs = 86400 split into kAuctionSlotTimeIntervals = 20, kVoteMaxSlots = 8 (include/xrpl/protocol/AMMCore.h). See ch03 DeFi. - PriceOracle — OracleSet (src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/oracle/OracleSet.cpp) for freshness-checked prices driving rebalancing decisions. - OfferCreate (src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/dex/OfferCreate.cpp) for order-book execution alongside AMM.

    Runbook. Read oracle price and check freshness → compute target allocation → rebalance via AMMDeposit/AMMWithdraw or OfferCreate with slippage bounds → monitor LP position for impermanent loss against thresholds → record every action against the validated ledger for audit.

    Example flow

    End-to-end cross-border payment (USD → EUR)

    This traces the cross-border architecture (§1) from a customer's fiat deposit to the beneficiary payout, showing where each on-ledger primitive and each failure path fits.

    1. Fiat deposit (off-ledger). The customer wires USD to the provider's bank. The provider records an internal credit but treats nothing as settled until the on-ledger leg validates.
    2. Construct the payment. Build a cross-currency Payment (src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/payment/Payment.cpp) from the operational account: Destination = the payout account, a DestinationTag identifying the beneficiary, a bounded SendMax in the source asset, the target Amount in the destination asset, and a LastLedgerSequence a few ledgers ahead for deterministic expiry. Set tfPartialPayment (0x00020000, include/xrpl/protocol/TxFlags.h:105) only if a short delivery is acceptable and priced accordingly.
    3. Submit and observe. Pathfinding routes across DEX order books and AMM pools (see ch03 DeFi). Submit, then poll the validated ledger — not the current one — for the transaction result.
    4. Read what was actually delivered. On success, read the metadata field delivered_amount (include/xrpl/protocol/jss.h:216), never the requested Amount. With tfPartialPayment, the two can differ; crediting the requested amount instead of the delivered amount is the classic exploit.
    5. Payout (off-ledger). Release EUR to the beneficiary only after the on-ledger delivery is validated and delivered_amount is confirmed.
    6. Failure paths. tecPATH_DRY / tecPATH_PARTIAL mean liquidity was insufficient — alert, surface a human-readable error, and re-quote rather than blindly retrying. If LastLedgerSequence passes with no result, the transaction expired safely and can be rebuilt at a fresh sequence (idempotent by hash — see ch01 Payments).
    7. Reconcile. On a schedule, reconcile fiat in/out against validated delivered_amount totals; any gap is an alert, not a rounding note.

    Why the validated ledger is the pivot: every "settled" decision in this flow hangs on a validated result and the metadata delivered amount. Trusting the submitted amount, the current (unvalidated) ledger, or an off-ledger assumption is where cross-border integrations lose money.

    Security considerations

    • Each architecture includes a threat model: which accounts can be compromised, what the blast radius is, and how to recover.
    • Monitoring and alerting must cover both on-ledger events (account activity, balance changes) and off-ledger state (reconciliation gaps, reserve shortfalls).
    • All operational accounts follow the security model in Security.

    Common mistakes

    • Missing reconciliation. Treating on-ledger state as always consistent with off-ledger databases without a reconciliation process leads to silent discrepancies that compound over time.
    • No monitoring on operational accounts. The ledger is live 24/7; unmonitored accounts are undefended accounts.
    • Single-account topologies. Combining issuer, hot wallet, and operational roles in one account maximizes blast radius.
    • Untested recovery procedures. Failure recovery paths that have never been executed will fail when they are needed most.

    Production checklist

    • [ ] Account topology diagram is documented and reflects live state.
    • [ ] Each account's role, signing authority, and reserve requirements are documented.
    • [ ] Transaction flows include explicit failure handling and retry logic.
    • [ ] Reconciliation runs on a defined schedule with alerting on gaps.
    • [ ] On-ledger monitoring covers all operational accounts.
    • [ ] Recovery procedures are documented and have been drilled.
    • [ ] Off-ledger systems treat the validated ledger as the source of truth.

    What I learned

    • Reference architectures are compositions, not new primitives. Every one of the five reuses transactors and flags documented in ch01–ch04; the value is in the account topology and ordering, not in any single on-ledger feature.
    • The validated ledger is the only source of truth, in all five. Deposit crediting, payout release, reserve reconciliation, and treasury audit all hang on a validated result and, for payments, the metadata delivered_amount. The recurring failure mode is trusting a submitted amount or an unvalidated ledger.
    • Account topology is a security decision. Splitting issuer/hot/cold and cold/hot/deposit roles bounds blast radius; the protocol's disable-master and signer-list guards (ch04) are what make those splits safe to operate.
    • The protocol enforces gates, not sequences. Order-of-operations in the RWA and stablecoin runbooks (identity → domain → issuance, or ceremony ordering) is an application-level discipline; the ledger enforces only the individual preconditions (tecOWNERS, tecNO_ALTERNATIVE_KEY, tecHAS_OBLIGATIONS).

    Open questions

    When should a design use separate accounts versus DestinationTags on one account?

    Educated guess (operational trade-off, not protocol-enforced): use a single account with DestinationTags when the sub-entities share a trust and compliance boundary (e.g. many customers of one custodian) — it minimizes reserve cost and key management. Use separate accounts when roles differ in signing authority or blast radius (issuer vs. hot vs. cold), where the point is isolation, not accounting. The protocol supports both; the deciding factor is who must be able to sign and what a compromise would cost.

    How much of a production system's correctness can the ledger guarantee?

    Confirmed against source (on-ledger half) + educated guess (off-ledger half): the ledger guarantees the on-ledger invariants each transactor enforces — authorization, obligations, key availability, AMM balance. It cannot guarantee that off-ledger fiat, reserves, or databases agree with on-ledger state; that is what reconciliation exists to catch. Every one of the five architectures therefore pairs an on-ledger flow with an off-ledger reconciliation loop, and the boundary between them is where production bugs concentrate.

    Payments

    Why does this matter?

    Payments are the XRPL's foundational use case. The ledger provides 3–5 second deterministic finality, atomic cross-currency settlement, and native pathfinding — capabilities that typically require multiple intermediaries and days of settlement in traditional finance. Getting payment logic right means understanding not just the happy path but every failure mode and how to recover from it safely.

    Mental model

    A payment is an atomic state transition: the sender's balance decreases, the receiver's increases, and any intermediate currency conversion and liquidity routing happen in the same transaction — or none of it does.

    There is no "pending" state in a validated payment. Either the full delivery occurred, or it did not.

    Key distinctions:

    • Delivered amount vs. send amount. For cross-currency and partial payments, the amount delivered may differ from the amount sent. Always read delivered_amount from the transaction metadata — never the Amount field alone.
    • Direct vs. rippled payments. Direct XRP payments require no trust line. Issued currency payments require trust lines and may traverse multiple hops through the order book or AMM.

    Architecture

    • Direct payments — XRP or same-currency IOU; single hop, minimal complexity. For XRP, no trust line required. For IOUs, both parties need a trust line to the same issuer (or the payment ripples through one).
    • Cross-currency payments — pathfinding (ripple_path_find for streaming, find_path for one-shot) discovers routes through the DEX order book and/or AMM pools. Settlement is fully atomic — the entire conversion and delivery succeed or nothing moves. The liquidity these payments consume — order-book offers, AMM pools, and how pathfinding composes them — is documented in Markets and Liquidity → DeFi.
    • Partial payments (tfPartialPayment) — delivers as much as possible up to the specified maximum Amount. Used legitimately for return payments (to avoid dust left by fees) but a common accounting exploit vector. Use tfLimitQuality to reject paths below a minimum exchange rate.
    • Payment channels — off-ledger signing of incremental signed claims; the channel is funded on-ledger and settled on-ledger. Full lifecycle:
    • PaymentChannelCreate — open and fund the channel.
    • Sender issues signed off-ledger claims (no transaction per claim).
    • Receiver submits PaymentChannelClaim to redeem the latest claim.
    • Either party closes with PaymentChannelClaim (with tfClose) or the sender reclaims after expiry with PaymentChannelClaim (with tfRenew). Suited for high-frequency micropayment streams where per-claim latency must be sub-second.
    • Escrow — conditional or time-locked XRP delivery.
    • Time-based: funds release after FinishAfter and before CancelAfter.
    • Condition-based: funds release when the submitter provides a PREIMAGE-SHA-256 crypto-condition fulfillment.
    • Escrows lock the full XRP amount plus owner reserve for the duration.
    • Checks — a deferred pull-payment primitive (analogous to a paper check). The sender creates a Check authorizing the receiver to pull up to a specified amount. The receiver cashes it with CheckCash; either party can cancel it. Useful for authorized payment flows where the receiver initiates settlement.

    Fee escalation and the transaction queue

    The required fee is not a single "base × load" number. Two distinct mechanisms interact, and a robust submitter must account for both:

    • Load-based fee. The server-wide load multiplier. Read load_factor from server_info and multiply by base_fee_xrp. This is the classic base_fee × load_factor.
    • Open-ledger fee escalation. Independent of load. There is a base fee level of 256 and an adaptive limit on how many transactions fit in the open ledger at that base level (at least 5, growing ~20% per healthy ledger up to 50, then tracking recent large ledgers; clamping down 50% when consensus is unhealthy). Once the open ledger exceeds the limit, the required fee level jumps quadratically: lastLedgerMedianFeeLevel × TxInOpenLedger² / limit². Read load_factor_fee_escalation (the openLedgerFeeLevel) from server_info; a burst can push this far above the load-based fee. Pay the max of the two to guarantee immediate inclusion.
    • Transaction queue (TxQ). A transaction that clears the base fee level and the load minimum but not the escalated open-ledger fee is queued and broadcast, not rejected. When the next ledger opens, queued transactions apply highest-fee-level first. A queued transaction is not a failure — it may still be applied in a later ledger. It leaves the queue only when it applies, fails, retries 10 times, its LastLedgerSequence expires, it is replaced by a same-sequence transaction paying ≥25% more, or it is dropped when the queue fills. Per account, at most 10 sequential transactions may be queued.

    Operational implication: a "pending" transaction is often queued, not lost. Distinguish "queued" from "expired/failed" before rebuilding and resubmitting.

    Example flow

    Direct XRP payment — the baseline

    Before tackling cross-currency, internalize this simpler case. Everything here maps directly to the transaction lifecycle in the mental models section.

    {
  "TransactionType": "Payment",
  "Account": "rAlice...",
  "Destination": "rBob...",
  "DestinationTag": 12345,
  "Amount": "10000000",        // 10 XRP in drops (1 XRP = 1,000,000 drops)
  "Fee": "12",                 // fetched from server_info, not hard-coded
  "Sequence": 47,
  "LastLedgerSequence": 91234  // current validated ledger + ~4
}

    Key points: - Amount for XRP is always a string integer in drops. Never use decimals. - DestinationTag is required if rBob's account has RequireDestTag set; omitting it returns tecDST_TAG_NEEDED and the fee is charged. - Fee must be fetched dynamically from server_info.load_factor × server_info.base_fee_xrp.

    Validated success metadata: 

    {
  "TransactionResult": "tesSUCCESS",
  "delivered_amount": "10000000"
}

    Validated failure metadata (e.g. insufficient XRP): 

    {
  "TransactionResult": "tecUNFUNDED_PAYMENT",
  "delivered_amount": "unavailable"
}
    The fee is charged even on tec failures. State did not change, but the sequence number advanced and the fee burned.

    Cross-currency payment: USD → EUR, atomic on-ledger settlement

    Scenario: Alice holds RLUSD (USD stablecoin) and wants to pay Bob, who expects EUR-denominated IOUs. Both currencies are live on the XRPL DEX.

    Step 1 — Pathfinding

    // Request: find routes from Alice's RLUSD to Bob's EUR trust line
{
  "command": "ripple_path_find",
  "source_account": "Alice",
  "destination_account": "Bob",
  "destination_amount": { "currency": "EUR", "issuer": "rEURIssuer", "value": "100" }
}
// Response includes candidate paths through DEX order books and/or AMM pools

    Step 2 — Build the transaction

    {
  "TransactionType": "Payment",
  "Account": "Alice",
  "Destination": "Bob",
  "Amount": {
    "currency": "EUR",
    "issuer": "rEURIssuer",
    "value": "100"
  },
  "SendMax": {
    "currency": "USD",
    "issuer": "rUSDIssuer",
    "value": "112"              // Alice is willing to spend up to 112 USD
  },
  "Paths": [ /* paths from ripple_path_find */ ],
  "Fee": "12",
  "Sequence": 47,
  "LastLedgerSequence": 91234   // current ledger + ~4
}

    Step 3 — Submit and wait for validation

    Alice signs → submits → node broadcasts → consensus → ledger validated

    Step 4 — Inspect metadata

    // From the validated tx metadata:
{
  "TransactionResult": "tesSUCCESS",
  "delivered_amount": {
    "currency": "EUR",
    "issuer": "rEURIssuer",
    "value": "100"             // exactly 100 EUR delivered
  }
}
// AffectedNodes shows: Alice's USD balance decreased, Bob's EUR balance
// increased, and intermediate offer/AMM objects consumed — all atomically.

    If liquidity was insufficient for the full 100 EUR, the transaction returns tecPATH_DRY (no path) or tecPATH_PARTIAL (partial fill not allowed without tfPartialPayment). Nothing moves in either case.

    Reliable submission loop

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Build : start
    Build --> Sign : autofill sequence + fee
    Sign --> Submit : sign offline

    Submit --> PollValidated : preliminary response (any code)

    PollValidated --> Done : tx found in validated ledger\nread meta.TransactionResult\n+ meta.delivered_amount
    PollValidated --> Expired : not found AND\ncurrent_ledger > LastLedgerSequence

    Expired --> Build : increment sequence\nrebuild + re-sign

    Done --> [*]

    note right of PollValidated
        Poll every ~1 s.
        Never resubmit before
        checking the validated ledger.
    end note

    Never resubmit without verifying the original tx is absent from the validated ledger. Resubmitting a transaction that already succeeded is wasteful; with a different Amount it is dangerous.

    Try it yourself

    Using the Testnet faucet at https://xrpl.org/resources/dev-tools/xrp-faucets:

    1. Run examples/02-reliable-submission/ — observe the polling loop. Kill the process mid-poll and restart; does it correctly detect the already-validated transaction or resubmit?
    2. Submit a cross-currency payment (USD→EUR) without any AMM pool or DEX offers in the path. What result code do you get? Is the fee charged?
    3. Submit a payment with tfPartialPayment and insufficient liquidity. Check meta.delivered_amount — is it the full Amount or less?
    4. Deliberately use a past LastLedgerSequence (e.g., current − 10). What happens? Does the node reject it immediately or after submission?
    5. Open a payment channel with a 100 XRP capacity. Issue three off-ledger claims for 10 XRP each. Submit the highest claim via PaymentChannelClaim — what balance does the receiver actually get?

    Security considerations

    • Always set LastLedgerSequence. Without it, a transaction can remain pending indefinitely — safe retry logic is impossible.
    • Never trust tesSUCCESS from the submission response. It is a prediction. Only the validated ledger is authoritative.
    • Validate delivered_amount in every ingestion pipeline. Partial payments exploit systems that credit Amount rather than the actual delivered amount. This is a documented attack vector.
    • Enforce RequireDestTag on all custodial accounts. Any account that uses destination tags for user attribution must set this flag. Without it, a payment with no tag will succeed and land in a limbo state in your reconciliation system.
    • Manage sequence numbers carefully under concurrent load. If two threads submit transactions for the same account simultaneously, both may use the same sequence number. Only one will succeed; the other returns tefPAST_SEQ or terPRE_SEQ. Use a sequence-number dispenser with a mutex or queue.
    • Payment channel settlement risk. If a receiver stops cooperating, the sender can reclaim channel funds only after the Expiration or CancelAfter time. Design channels with appropriate expiry windows and a tested reclaim runbook.

    Common mistakes

    • Reading Amount instead of delivered_amount. For cross-currency and partial payments this causes significant accounting errors.
    • Omitting LastLedgerSequence. Transactions without an expiry can be applied to the ledger arbitrarily far in the future.
    • Floating-point amounts. XRPL amounts are exact. Use string or integer representations throughout.
    • Not handling tecPATH_DRY and tecPATH_PARTIAL. Liquidity is not guaranteed; applications must handle and retry or surface these errors.
    • Ignoring destination tags. Exchanges and custodians rely on destination tags for attribution; silently dropping them breaks reconciliation.
    • Hard-coding fees. The required fee scales under load and under open-ledger congestion. A hard-coded "12" drop fee will be queued or dropped during a spike. Compute the fee as the max of the load-based fee (base_fee_xrp × load_factor) and the escalated open-ledger fee (load_factor_fee_escalation), both from server_info.
    • Treating a queued transaction as failed. A transaction that lands in the TxQ is still live — resubmitting a fresh one with a new sequence can create duplicates or a tefPAST_SEQ cascade. Poll for the queued transaction (or replace it with a ≥25% higher fee at the same sequence) instead of blindly rebuilding.
    • Misunderstanding SendMax. SendMax is the maximum the sender is willing to spend — not the exact amount. If liquidity allows a better rate, the sender spends less than SendMax. Applications that treat it as an exact debit will overstate costs.
    • Using ripple_path_find when find_path is sufficient. ripple_path_find opens a streaming subscription that sends path updates every ledger close. For one-shot payment construction, use find_path to avoid holding an open subscription and leaking connections.
    • Race condition on account sequence under concurrent submissions. Two goroutines/threads submitting transactions for the same account will collide on the sequence number. One will succeed; the other must detect tefPAST_SEQ and retry with the correct sequence fetched from the validated ledger.

    Production checklist

    • [ ] Every transaction has a valid LastLedgerSequence.
    • [ ] All payment results are verified against the validated ledger.
    • [ ] delivered_amount is read from metadata — not Amount.
    • [ ] Partial payment flag (tfPartialPayment) detected and handled in all ingestion pipelines.
    • [ ] Submission loop is safe to retry without double-spending (hash-based idempotency check before resubmit).
    • [ ] Destination tag policy defined and enforced; RequireDestTag flag set on all custodial accounts.
    • [ ] Path errors (tecPATH_DRY, tecPATH_PARTIAL) trigger alerting and a human-readable error surface for the end user.
    • [ ] Fee calculated dynamically as max(base_fee_xrp × load_factor, load_factor_fee_escalation) from server_info — no hard-coded fee constants in transaction construction code.
    • [ ] Submission logic distinguishes queued from expired/failed before resubmitting; replacement uses ≥25% higher fee at the same sequence.
    • [ ] Account sequence number dispenser is concurrency-safe (mutex, queue, or atomic counter) under concurrent transaction submission.
    • [ ] Stuck transaction monitoring in place: alert if any account's in-flight transaction is not confirmed within N ledger closes.
    • [ ] Payment channel settlement runbook written and tested: how to reclaim funds if the counterparty goes offline or becomes uncooperative.
    • [ ] Escrow expiry windows and cancel-after times tested on Testnet before any mainnet deployment.

    This checklist covers the payment path. Before production, also complete the account-security checklist in ch04-hardening/security and the node-operations checklist in ch04-hardening/operations.

    What I learned

    • delivered_amount is the only number that matters. The Amount field is what the sender requested. For cross-currency and partial payments, the actual settlement amount can be lower. Ingestion pipelines that read Amount will silently misbook balances.
    • LastLedgerSequence is your expiry valve. It turns an open-ended "maybe it lands eventually" problem into a deterministic "the transaction is dead after ledger N" guarantee. Without it, safe retry logic is impossible.
    • Pathfinding is advisory, not guaranteed. Routes returned by ripple_path_find are a snapshot of current liquidity. By the time your transaction is applied, offers may have moved. Design for tecPATH_DRY and tecPATH_PARTIAL as normal operating conditions, not exceptional ones.
    • x402 inverts the payment flow. Traditional payment APIs pull funds server-side. x402 embeds a signed payment authorization in an HTTP 402 challenge/response cycle — the client proves payment before the server serves the resource. This is composable with any XRPL payment primitive.
    • RLUSD is the preferred bridge currency. For corridors with thin native asset liquidity, RLUSD provides a USD-denominated settlement path without requiring a direct trust line between sender and receiver. It reduces pathfinding complexity and slippage in cross-currency flows.

    Open questions

    Why is the XRPL uniquely suited for high-throughput payment systems compared to traditional rails and other L1 networks?

    Hypothesis: Three properties compound to make XRPL structurally superior for payments. First, deterministic 3–5 second finality — SWIFT takes 1–5 business days; Bitcoin requires 60+ minutes for probabilistic confidence; XRPL settles once and permanently. Second, native atomic cross-currency settlement — no correspondent banking, no nostro/vostro pre-funding, no multi-leg settlement risk; the full conversion happens in a single transaction. Third, sub-cent fees that burn — at ~0.00001 XRP per transaction, fees are economically irrelevant for most payment sizes, and they don't accumulate to a validator rent-seeking problem. Compared to Ethereum (~15–30 TPS, probabilistic finality, gas fees that spike under load) and Solana (higher TPS but validator-concentrated and prone to outages), XRPL's consensus mechanism is specifically optimized for financial settlement: low latency, low variance, high availability.

    How does atomic cross-currency settlement actually work at the ledger level — what prevents partial fills from leaving state inconsistent?

    Confirmed against source (rippled/include/xrpl/ledger/PaymentSandbox.h, rippled/src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/payment/Payment.cpp): When a Payment with Paths is applied, the XRPL transaction engine executes the entire path as a single atomic state transition using a PaymentSandbox — a transactional view that records tentative balance changes without committing them. At each hop, the engine identifies the best available liquidity, computes the exchange, and records tentative changes in the sandbox. Only if every hop succeeds end-to-end are all changes committed to the ledger simultaneously via PaymentSandbox::apply(). If any hop fails — insufficient liquidity, trust line limit hit, quality below tfLimitQuality threshold — the engine returns tecPATH_DRY or tecPATH_PARTIAL and the sandbox is discarded. The fee is charged and the sequence advances, but no balances move. It is impossible for a cross-currency payment to leave the ledger in a half-converted state.

    For how the engine models each hop — order-book offers and AMM pools competing as interchangeable liquidity via synthetic AMM offers — see Markets and Liquidity → DeFi.

    What are all the failure modes for payments and the correct recovery path for each?

    Confirmed against source (rippled/include/xrpl/protocol/TER.h): Failure modes fall into three categories:

    Liquidity failures (tec — fee charged, sequence advances): - tecPATH_DRY — no path with sufficient liquidity; retry later or with a higher SendMax. - tecPATH_PARTIAL — partial fill possible but tfPartialPayment not set; add the flag or wait for liquidity to improve. - tecUNFUNDED_PAYMENT — sender balance insufficient; top up and retry.

    Destination failures (tec): - tecNO_DST — destination account does not exist; fund it with at least base_reserve XRP first. - tecDST_TAG_NEEDED — destination requires a tag; always include it. - tecNO_LINE_INSUF_RESERVE — no trust line and recipient can't create one (reserve exhausted); recipient must free reserve or top up.

    Submission / sequence failures (ter / tef — fee NOT charged, sequence unchanged): - terPRE_SEQ — sequence too high (gap exists); fill the gap or fetch current sequence and rebuild. - tefPAST_SEQ — sequence already consumed; fetch current sequence and retry. - terINSUF_FEE_B — account balance cannot cover the fee (the account's XRP balance is literally too low to pay even the base fee); top up the account. Note: telINSUF_FEE_P is the distinct local-node error when the offered fee is below the node's current load threshold — the fix for that case is to fetch server_info and recalculate the fee amount.

    Expiry: - Transaction not found after LastLedgerSequence closes → permanently expired; safe to rebuild with fresh sequence and LastLedgerSequence.

    Network partition: - Node unreachable during submission → cannot confirm broadcast; poll a different node for the transaction hash before deciding to resubmit.

    What is the x402 protocol, and how does embedding payment authorization in HTTP headers change the architecture of monetized APIs?

    External ecosystem pattern — not protocol-encoded in rippled. Source: x402.org. No rippled source verification possible or needed. x402 is an extension of the HTTP 402 Payment Required status code into a machine-to-machine payment flow. The server responds to a request with a 402 containing a payment payload (amount, destination address, network, expiry). The client constructs and signs an XRPL payment, then retries the request with a X-Payment header containing the signed transaction. The server verifies on-chain inclusion before serving the resource. The architectural implication is a fundamental inversion of the traditional pull-payment model: instead of the server initiating a charge against stored credentials (card, ACH), the client proves payment inline in the HTTP request. This eliminates the need for API keys, subscriptions, and billing infrastructure for the common case of pay-per-use access. It also makes payment composable with any client — an AI agent, a CLI tool, or a browser — without requiring account registration. The main constraint is that it requires the client to hold an XRPL account and manage keys, which limits applicability to machine-to-machine and developer-facing APIs today.

    What specific role does RLUSD play in the payment ecosystem — as a bridge currency, a settlement asset, and a liquidity anchor for cross-corridor flows?

    Confirmed (ecosystem-level, not protocol-encoded). The pathfinding engine treats RLUSD as any other IOU; its bridge/settlement/anchor roles are operational advantages, not enforced by xrpld. RLUSD plays three compounding roles. As a bridge currency, it reduces the trust-line combinatorics of cross-currency payments: instead of needing a direct IOU trust line between every sender-currency/receiver- currency pair, corridors converge on RLUSD as a common intermediate, so N currencies need only N RLUSD pairs rather than N×(N−1)/2 bilateral pairs. As a settlement asset, it provides USD-denominated atomic finality in 3–5 seconds — replacing the pre-funded nostro/vostro accounts that correspondent banks require to provide same-day settlement. As a liquidity anchor, RLUSD/XRP and RLUSD/IOU AMM pools provide reliable, deep routing for the pathfinding engine; thin corridors that would otherwise return tecPATH_DRY become viable when RLUSD is an intermediate hop. The compliance dimension also matters: Ripple maintains KYC/AML controls on RLUSD issuance, making it acceptable for regulated payment corridors that cannot use unattested issued currencies.

    How do payment channels change the throughput and latency profile for high-frequency micropayment use cases, and what are their settlement risks?

    Confirmed against source (rippled/src/libxrpl/tx/transactors/payment/ PaymentChannelCreate.cpp, PaymentChannelClaim.cpp): Payment channels decouple the per-payment cost and latency from on-ledger transaction fees. Once a channel is open and funded (one on-ledger transaction), the sender issues signed off-ledger claims for each micropayment — no consensus, no fee per claim, latency limited only by network round-trip between the two parties (sub-100 ms). Throughput is theoretically unlimited within the funded capacity. The trade-offs are settlement risk on both sides: the receiver holds signed claims that have not yet been cashed, so the sender's on-ledger balance is the backstop — if the sender drains their account before the receiver cashes, the claim may fail. Conversely, if the receiver stops cooperating, the sender must wait for the channel's Expiration or CancelAfter time to reclaim funds. The mitigations are: (1) receiver should cash claims periodically — not just at channel close — to bound exposure; (2) sender should not set excessively long expiry windows; (3) both parties should test the reclaim path on Testnet before going live. Channels are best suited for streaming payments between known, trusted counterparties (e.g., API metering, content streaming) rather than one-shot payments to unknown recipients.

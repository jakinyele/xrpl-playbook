    DeFi

    Why does this matter?

    The XRPL's DeFi primitives — the native order book DEX and the AMM — are built into the protocol, not smart contracts. This means they compose atomically with payments and require no external contracts to audit. But it also means liquidity behavior, fee mechanics, and execution guarantees differ fundamentally from EVM-based DeFi. Practitioners must understand these mechanics before building routing, liquidity provision, or treasury management on top of them.

    Mental model

    Two complementary liquidity mechanisms, both native to the protocol:

    • DEX (order book) — a central limit order book where offers are on-ledger objects. Offers cross automatically when placed; unfilled orders rest in the book. Auto-bridging routes through XRP when it improves execution price.
    • AMM — a pool using a geometric mean invariant: the LP token supply is anchored to sqrt(asset1 × asset2) (ammLPTokens in AMMHelpers.cpp computes root2(asset1 * asset2), with the on-ledger invariant LPTokenBalance ≤ sqrt(asset1 × asset2)), which behaves like a constant-product market maker for marginal trades. Each AMM is a ledger account (ltAMM) holding two assets (sfAsset/sfAsset2); LP tokens represent proportional ownership. The protocol charges a trading fee (sfTradingFee) set at pool creation and adjustable via a governance vote (AMMVote, up to kVoteMaxSlots = 8 weighted votes). The fee is capped at kTradingFeeThreshold = 1000, i.e. 1% (fee units are 1/100000, so 1 = 0.001%); there is no protocol-mandated default.

    Pathfinding combines both: a cross-currency payment may route through DEX order book hops, AMM pools, or both in a single atomic transaction. This liquidity is not consumed in isolation — it is what Foundations → Payments routes through when settling a cross-currency Payment; the atomicity and tecPATH_DRY/tecPATH_PARTIAL failure modes described there are the payment-side view of the mechanics detailed below.

    Architecture

    • Offers — on-ledger objects specifying an exchange rate and quantity. Passive offers avoid crossing on placement; fill-or-kill offers expire immediately if not filled.
    • AMM pools — one pool per asset pair; a protocol-owned account holds the reserves and mints LP tokens on deposit.
    • Liquidity provision — double-asset deposits maintain pool ratio; single-asset deposits incur an implicit swap cost.
    • Continuous auction mechanism — the AMM's sfAuctionSlot lets one LP win the slot (via AMMBid) and trade at a discounted fee for a 24-hour window (kTotalTimeSlotSecs), incentivizing active management. The slot holder pays the discounted fraction kAuctionSlotDiscountedFeeFraction = 1/10 of the pool fee; the window is divided into kAuctionSlotTimeIntervals = 20 decay intervals, and up to kAuctionSlotMaxAuthAccounts = 4 additional accounts can share the discount.
    • Oracles — on-ledger price oracle objects aggregate off-chain feeds; consumers must account for staleness and aggregation methodology.

    Example flow

    AMM Swap via Pathfinding

    1. Transaction Submission: A user submits a Payment transaction with tfPartialPayment and a specified Paths array (calculated via ripple_path_find).
    2. Path Selection: The ledger's pathfinding engine evaluates the cheapest route:
    3. Route A: XRP/USD DEX Offer (order book).
    4. Route B: XRP/USD AMM Pool (automated liquidity).
    5. Invariant Check: The AMM calculates the swap impact from the constant-product relationship. Illustratively (ignoring fees), the output is asset2_out = asset2_bal - (asset1_bal * asset2_bal) / (asset1_bal + asset1_in); the exact xrpld formulas (AMMHelpers.cpp, Equations 3/4/7/8) fold in the fee multipliers f1 = 1 - tfee and f2 = (1 - tfee/2)/f1.
    6. Execution: If Route B is cheaper, the AMM reserves are updated: asset1 increases, asset2 decreases, and the trading fee (set per pool, e.g. 0.3%) is retained in the pool reserves, effectively increasing the value of all LP tokens.
    7. Finality: The transaction completes atomically; the user receives USD, and the AMM pool state is updated in a single ledger close.

    Try it yourself

    Using the Testnet faucet at https://xrpl.org/resources/dev-tools/xrp-faucets:

    1. Run examples/04-amm-deposit/ — observe the LP token minted after creating the AMM. Then compute the expected token count using the formula sqrt(asset1 × asset2) and compare.
    2. Submit a cross-currency Payment from XRP to your USD issuer without specifying Paths. Does pathfinding route through the AMM automatically?
    3. Set DeliverMin on a payment to reject partial fills. Try with and without tfPartialPayment and observe the result codes.
    4. Use ripple_path_find to see what routes the engine proposes for a USD→EUR swap. Does it suggest DEX hops, AMM hops, or both?
    5. Bid on the auction slot with AMMBid and verify your account appears in the AuctionSlot.AuthAccounts field of the amm_info response.

    Security considerations

    • AMM prices can be manipulated within a single ledger close; use TWAP or multi-source oracles for any on-chain pricing logic.
    • LP positions are exposed to impermanent loss when asset prices diverge; model this explicitly in treasury strategies.
    • Offers resting in the book are visible to all participants; large orders can be front-run by higher-fee transactions in the same ledger close.
    • Oracle data has a freshness timestamp; never consume a price without validating it against an acceptable staleness threshold.

    Common mistakes

    • Ignoring auto-bridging. Routes through XRP can provide better execution than direct pairs; pathfinding must be allowed to explore XRP-bridged routes.
    • Not accounting for slippage in AMM swaps. The geometric-mean invariant means large swaps move the price significantly; set SendMax or a slippage tolerance appropriately.
    • Single-asset AMM deposits without modeling the swap cost. Single-asset deposits implicitly swap half the deposit to rebalance the pool, incurring a fee and price impact.
    • Stale oracle consumption. Using a price without a freshness check can cause mispriced liquidations or incorrect valuations.

    Production checklist

    • [ ] Pathfinding is configured to explore both DEX and AMM routes.
    • [ ] Slippage tolerance is set on all market-taking transactions.
    • [ ] Oracle prices are validated against a freshness threshold before use.
    • [ ] LP positions are monitored for impermanent loss against rebalancing thresholds.
    • [ ] AMM trading fees and auction slot status are tracked for LP positions.
    • [ ] Large orders are split or timed to minimize price impact.

    This checklist covers markets and liquidity. Before production, also complete the account-security checklist in ch04-hardening/security and the node-operations checklist in ch04-hardening/operations.

    What I learned

    • Protocol-native DeFi is safer but more rigid. You cannot "hot-fix" an AMM bug like you can with a smart contract, but you also don't have to worry about re-entrancy attacks or contract-level vulnerabilities.
    • LP tokens are trust-line IOUs. AMM LP tokens are issued by the AMM account and held as RippleState (trust line) balances; the pool's total is tracked in sfLPTokenBalance on the ltAMM object. (An MPT-based variant is only relevant once MPTokensV2 activates; it is not on Mainnet today.)
    • The trading fee is capped at 1%, with no protocol default. 0.3% is a common convention, not a hardwired value; the protocol only enforces the kTradingFeeThreshold = 1000 (1%) ceiling. The continuous auction slot can significantly change the yield profile for active LPs by discounting that fee to 1/10 for the slot holder.
    • Pathfinding is the "invisible hand." It doesn't matter if liquidity is in the DEX or AMM; the protocol will find it.

    Open questions

    What are the mechanics of the native Decentralized Exchange (DEX) vs. the Automated Market Maker (AMM)?

    They are two distinct liquidity structures that pathfinding treats interchangeably, but their execution models differ fundamentally:

    • DEX (central limit order book). Liquidity is discrete: each Offer is an on-ledger object at a fixed exchange rate and quantity. An incoming offer consumes resting offers in price-priority order until it is filled or its limit price is exhausted; anything left rests in the book. Price is whatever counterparties posted — there is no formula. tfPassive avoids crossing on placement; fill-or-kill / immediate-or-cancel variants control partial execution. Auto-bridging synthesizes an XRP hop between two IOU books when that improves the effective rate.
    • AMM (constant-function pool). Liquidity is continuous: a single ltAMM pool of two assets prices every trade off the geometric-mean invariant. There are no counterparties — the pool is the counterparty — and the marginal price moves with every unit traded (slippage is a function of trade size relative to pool depth). LPs earn the sfTradingFee, which stays in the pool and accrues to LP-token value.
    • How they compose. An AMM does not rest orders in the book. Instead, during payment execution the engine synthesizes a virtual offer from the pool (AMMOffer/AMMLiquidity) and lets it compete with real order-book offers hop-by-hop. A single Payment can therefore consume order-book offers and AMM liquidity in the same atomic transaction, always taking the best-quality slice first. See the worked cross-currency example in Foundations → Payments for the payment-side view of this same flow.

    Confirmed against source — order-book stepping and auto-bridging in the dex/OfferCreate.cpp transactor and tx/paths book steps; AMM virtual offers in tx/paths/AMMOffer.cpp and AMMLiquidity.cpp; the geometric-mean invariant in ammLPTokens (AMMHelpers.cpp).

    How do lending protocols and vaults leverage XRPL's native liquidity?

    Through two composable ledger objects that sit above the DEX/AMM rather than inside them — currently code-complete but in the voting stage (SingleAssetVault and LendingProtocol are Supported::Yes / VoteBehavior::DefaultNo, so not yet active on Mainnet):

    • Vault (ltVAULT, XLS-65). A single-asset pool where many depositors aggregate liquidity. Deposits mint shares, tracked not as trust lines but as an MPT (sfShareMPTID points at an MPTokenIssuance) — a concrete reuse of the ch02 token primitive. The object tracks sfAssetsTotal, sfAssetsAvailable (idle vs. deployed capital), sfAssetsMaximum (a cap), sfLossUnrealized, and a sfWithdrawalPolicy.
    • Lending (ltLOAN / ltLOAN_BROKER, XLS-66). A loan broker draws on a vault's available assets to originate loans; repayments (principal + interest, less fees) flow back to the vault, raising share value. The difference between sfAssetsTotal and sfAssetsAvailable is exactly the capital currently lent out.

    The "leverage native liquidity" linkage is indirect: vaults are a funding layer whose deposited assets can themselves be DEX/AMM-tradable IOUs, and access is gated by the same credential/permissioned-domain machinery from ch02 (only accounts satisfying a domain may participate). The protocol does not automatically route vault capital into AMM pools; composition is an application-level decision.

    Confirmed against source — Vault.h fields (sfShareMPTID, sfAssetsTotal, sfAssetsAvailable, sfAssetsMaximum, sfLossUnrealized, sfWithdrawalPolicy), Loan.h / LoanBroker.h, and the Supported::Yes / DefaultNo status in features.macro.

    How does the protocol handle liquidations and price stability?

    Educated guess (mostly application-level, not protocol-enforced). The XRPL core protocol has no built-in liquidation engine or price-stability mechanism the way a monolithic lending smart contract might. What the protocol does provide are primitives that applications compose into those behaviours:

    • Price inputs come from ltORACLE objects, which carry a sfPriceDataSeries and a sfLastUpdateTime but no protocol-enforced max-age — staleness policy is entirely the consumer's responsibility. Aggregation across providers is exposed via the get_aggregate_price RPC.
    • AMM spot price is manipulable within a single ledger, so any liquidation trigger reading an AMM mid-price directly is unsafe; robust designs read an oracle (or a TWAP) instead.
    • Liquidation execution, when the lending amendments activate, is expected to be driven by the loan/broker logic (comparing collateral value from an oracle against loan terms and seizing/selling collateral), not by a generic protocol liquidation path. The exact on-ledger enforcement lives in the LendingProtocol transactors, which are not yet active on Mainnet.
    • "Price stability" for stablecoins is handled by issuers (ch02) off-ledger — reserves, redemption, TransferRate, freeze/clawback — not by any ledger-level peg mechanism.

    The honest summary: the ledger supplies oracles, atomic settlement, and the AMM/DEX/vault primitives; liquidation and peg maintenance are assembled from those pieces at the application layer.
